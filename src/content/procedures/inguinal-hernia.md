<h2>Overview</h2>
      <p>An inguinal hernia occurs when abdominal contents protrude through a naturally occurring but unclosed passage in the groin (the processus vaginalis). It presents as an intermittent groin or scrotal/labial bulge, often more prominent with crying or straining. Unlike in adults, childhood inguinal hernias do not resolve on their own and require surgical repair to prevent incarceration.</p>
      <h2>The Procedure</h2>
      <p>Repair is performed under general anaesthesia through a small groin incision. The hernia sac is identified, separated from surrounding structures, and tied off at its origin. In selected cases a laparoscopic (keyhole) technique may be used.</p>
      <h2>Preparing for Surgery</h2>
      <p>Standard day-surgery preparation and fasting instructions apply. Repair is usually arranged promptly after diagnosis given the risk of incarceration.</p>
      <h2>Recovery</h2>
      <p>Most children go home the same day. Mild fussiness on waking is common and settles quickly. Normal activity can generally resume within a few days, with heavier activity avoided for two weeks.</p>
      <h2>Possible Complications</h2>
      <p>General surgical risks apply, along with a small risk of hernia recurrence and, rarely, injury to the blood vessels or vas deferens supplying the testis — this risk is slightly higher during repeat surgery. A hernia can occasionally develop later on the opposite side.</p>
      <h2>Follow-up</h2>
      <p>Review is arranged approximately three to four weeks after surgery.</p>
